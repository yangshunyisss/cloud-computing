1. What problem does serverless computing aim to solve compared to traditional microservice deployment on Kubernetes? Give one example where serverless is clearly better, and one where it may not be.
   Serverless reduces “always-on” capacity planning and ops toil (provisioning, scaling, patching) by autoscaling to meet demand—including scale-to-zero—and by shifting infra concerns to the platform with fine-grained, request-based billing.
   • Clearly better: bursty, spiky, or sporadic workloads—e.g., a thumbnail/AI inference endpoint triggered by uploads that sits idle most of the day. You pay near-zero when idle and scale out instantly under load.
   • May not be: latency-sensitive, stateful, or long-running jobs—e.g., a low-latency trading gateway or a streaming engine with warm state. Cold starts, request timeouts, and stateless constraints can be deal-breakers.

2. What are the advantages of using a service mesh (like Istio) for managing microservices communication instead of relying only on Kubernetes networking?
   • Zero-code traffic control: timeouts, retries, circuit breaking, request shaping without touching app code.
   • Security by default: mTLS between services, identity (SPIFFE/SPIRE), policy enforcement.
   • Uniform observability: consistent metrics, distributed traces, and access logs for every call.
   • Policy & governance: rate limits, authz, and RBAC at the mesh layer.
   • Progressive delivery: weighted routing, header-based splits, mirroring, and failover across versions.

3. Explain what a sidecar proxy (such as Envoy in Istio) does. Why is it needed in a service mesh?
   The sidecar intercepts all inbound/outbound traffic for a pod, applying mesh policy: mTLS handshakes, authN/Z, retries, circuit breaking, routing rules, and telemetry emission. It’s needed to:
   • Enforce consistent, centralized traffic policy without modifying application code.
   • Provide service identity and encryption transparently.
   • Emit standardized metrics/traces/logs for every request.
   This decouples app logic from networking concerns at the cost of some CPU/RAM and hop latency per request.

4. What kind of traffic management features does Istio provide? Give two examples of how they can be useful in production systems.
   Istio supports weighted routing, header/cookie-based routing, request mirroring, outlier detection, circuit breaking, retries with budgets, timeouts, fault injection, and mTLS.
   • Example 1 (canary rollout): send 5% of traffic to v2 and 95% to v1; increase gradually as SLOs hold.
   • Example 2 (safe experimentation): mirror 100% of production traffic to a new model/service for load and correctness checks without impacting users.

5. Explain how Knative Serving enables autoscaling for an application. What triggers scaling up and scaling down?
   Knative Serving adds a request-aware autoscaler (KPA by default; can delegate to HPA) with scale-to-zero:
   • Scale up triggers: rising concurrent requests or RPS per pod (measured by the queue-proxy); backlog growth.
   • Scale down triggers: sustained low concurrency; the activator/queue-proxy metrics show idleness and Knative shrinks pods—possibly to zero—if no requests arrive for a configured window.
   You can tune targets (e.g., `container-concurrency`, `autoscaling.knative.dev/target`) and min/max replicas.

6. What is the role of Knative Eventing, and how does it support event-driven architectures?
   Knative Eventing provides plumbing for producing, routing, and consuming CloudEvents. It offers:
   • Sources (e.g., Kafka, GitHub, HTTP) to ingest events.
   • Brokers/Triggers to decouple publishers from subscribers via attribute filters.
   • Channels/Subscriptions for fan-out and delivery guarantees.
   Apps become stateless, event-driven functions that scale with event volume and remain loosely coupled.

7. How does Knative leverage Kubernetes primitives to provide a serverless experience? Discuss which components of Kubernetes (e.g., Deployments, Services, Horizontal Pod Autoscaler) are abstracted away and how this abstraction benefits developers.
   Knative defines higher-level CRDs—`Service`, `Configuration`, `Route`, `Revision`—and compiles them into native K8s objects:
   • It creates/updates Deployments/ReplicaSets/Pods, Services, Endpoints, and (optionally) HPAs behind the scenes.
   • It configures Ingress (via Istio or another gateway), networking, and autoscaling policies automatically.
   • Revisions give immutable, versioned snapshots of your container + config for instant rollbacks and traffic splits.
   Benefit: developers focus on a single URL’d service and image; the platform handles rollouts, traffic shaping, autoscaling, and scale-to-zero—no YAML sprawl of Deployments/Services/HPAs required.

8. In KServe, what is the main function of an InferenceService, and how does it simplify deploying ML models?
   `InferenceService` is the top-level CRD that declares how a model is served (predictor, optional transformer and explainer) and desired compute. KServe turns this into a production-ready endpoint by wiring: model servers (e.g., Triton, TorchServe, SKLearn), autoscaling via Knative, networking via Istio, model download sidecars, health probes, and observability. You specify storage URI and runtime; KServe handles the rest.

9. In a production ML workflow using KServe, describe how data moves from an incoming HTTP request to a model prediction response. Which layers (Knative, Istio, KServe, Kubernetes) handle which responsibilities, and where could latency bottlenecks occur?
   • Client → Istio IngressGateway: terminates TLS, applies routing, retries, and policies (Istio).
   • Gateway → Knative: if the service is at zero, the Activator buffers initial requests and triggers scale-up (Knative).
   • Request → Pod’s queue-proxy → predictor container: queue-proxy records metrics and enforces concurrency; the predictor (KServe model server or custom) runs inference. Optional transformer/explainer run in-line (KServe/Knative).
   • Kubernetes: schedules pods to nodes, mounts secrets/volumes, and provides service discovery.
   • KServe Model Agent/Initializer: may fetch models from object storage at startup and keep them refreshed.
   Potential bottlenecks: cold starts (pod spin-up + image pull), model download/warmup, CPU/GPU initialization, transformer pre/post-processing, deserialization of large payloads, and sidecar hop overhead (Envoy, queue-proxy). Under load, queueing at the Activator or pod-level concurrency limits can add latency.

10. How can Istio’s traffic routing capabilities (e.g., weighted routing, retries, circuit breaking) be used to support canary deployments or A/B testing in Knative or KServe environments? Discuss the pros and cons compared to manual rollout strategies.
    Usage: define `DestinationRule` subsets (v1, v2) and a `VirtualService` with weights (e.g., 95/5). Knative Route percentages or KServe revision canaries map cleanly to those subsets. Add retries with budgets and circuit breaking to protect the baseline during experiments; use header-based routing or cookies for A/B cohorts; mirror traffic for dark launches.
    Pros vs manual rollouts (plain Deployments/RollingUpdate):
    • Fine-grained control: per-request weights, cohort routing, and easy instant rollback by flipping weights.
    • Safer experiments: mesh-level timeouts/retries/outlier detection reduce blast radius.
    • Rich telemetry: per-subset metrics and tracing accelerate decisions.
    Cons:
    • Complexity & drift: more CRDs/policies to manage; misconfig can cause blackholes or loops.
    • Overhead: sidecars add resource and minor latency overhead.
    • Operational maturity required: needs solid SLOs, alerting, and practice to run safely.
    Manual strategies are simpler and have fewer moving parts but offer coarser control and weaker guardrails; they’re fine for low-risk updates, less ideal for high-traffic, user-visible changes.
